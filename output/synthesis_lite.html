Narrative

The US economy is in an Overheat regime: growth is accelerating, inflation is accelerating, and the Fed is expected to hike rates further. This should be a risk-on environment, and indeed the S&P 500 is at a record high. However, beneath the surface, investors are buying protection. The demand for downside protection index has spiked, bond market volatility has jumped, and high-yield credit is showing cracks. This suggests that the market is starting to price in the risk that the Fed's tightening will eventually break something.

The key tension is between the strong economic data and the market's nervousness. The futures curve now prices two more hikes by mid-2027, a significant shift. This is hitting rate-sensitive assets and causing stress in emerging markets, as seen in the extreme Taiwan outflows. If the Fed continues to tighten into an overheating economy, the risk of a policy mistake rises. Investors should watch for any signs that the Fed is falling behind the curve or that inflation is becoming entrenched.

Trigger risk · If the Fed continues to hike rates into an overheating economy and inflation fails to cool, then risk assets could face a sharp correction as hedging costs spike and liquidity tightens.

Structural Backdrop
Where we stand in the big cycle

We are in a late-cycle Overheat phase, with the Fed tightening into an economy that is still running hot. The Fed's balance sheet is stable but net liquidity is draining, as bank reserves have fallen 3.6% over the past 13 weeks. The rate futures curve implies two more hikes by mid-2027, pushing the policy rate to 4.575%. The yield curve is flat at +0.34 pp, and its direction is unclear, which historically signals a transition period. This structural backdrop suggests that liquidity conditions will continue to tighten, favouring short-duration assets and defensive equities over long-duration bonds over a multi-year horizon.

Historical Context

The current setup most closely echoes mid-2007, when a flat yield curve, low volatility, and credit complacency preceded a sharp market correction. Back then, the Fed was tightening into a slowing economy, and investors were similarly complacent about risk. The key similarity today is the combination of record equity prices, tight credit spreads, and rising hedging costs. The most important difference is that today's inflation is much higher, and the Fed is hiking into an overheating economy rather than a weakening one. If this episode follows a similar path, we could see a sudden spike in volatility and a sharp drawdown in risk assets, but the stronger economic backdrop may limit the downside.
