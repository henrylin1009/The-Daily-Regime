S&P 500 hits record, volatility tumbles 17%

The S&P 500 reached a new all-time high of 7,431, while the VIX collapsed 17.5% this week to 17.8, its lowest in months. This extreme calm, alongside near-record tight credit spreads, shows investors are fully embracing the Goldilocks narrative.

US equities are at all-time highs, with the S&P 500 climbing to 7,431 and the VIX plunging 17.5% in a week to 17.8—signaling deep risk appetite. High-yield credit spreads remain near historic tights at just 1.55%, reflecting no fear of defaults. However, gold spiked 3.7% and oil dropped 3.9%, suggesting some rotation into safe havens. US

Goldilocks holds, but hedging costs flash warning

iThe S&P 500 sits at an all-time high (7,431) with a 20-day momentum z-score of 1.95, while the market volatility has fallen to 17.8 (52nd percentile) after a 17.5% weekly drop. Despite this, the GVZ (gold volatility) z-score is at 0.24 and deteriorating, and equity demand for downside protection is rising (z-score -0.6, deteriorating), showing that downside puts are getting bid. Credit spreads at 1.55% (9th percentile) reflect extreme complacency. Historically, such divergence between surface calm and hedging demand has preceded corrections, as seen in late 2018.

The US economy is in a perfect Goldilocks spot—growth accelerating and inflation cooling—pushing equities to records and volatility to the floor. Yet, the cost of insuring against gold price swings (GVZ) is spiking, and equity demand for downside protection shows growing demand for downside protection, a pattern often seen before market stress. This suggests that while retail and momentum traders are all-in, sophisticated money is quietly hedging.

Long-term takeaway · Maintain equity exposure but consider adding tail hedges via gold or volatility products to protect against a sudden regime shift.

Japan · US

Yen carry trade becomes extremely one-sided

iCFTC data shows yen bearish bets at -78,076 contracts, with a 52-week z-score of -2.61—in the tail of the distribution. The yen has weakened 1.14% against the dollar over the past month, widening the US-Japan rate differential to 197 basis points. Japanese holdings of US Treasuries have grown by $28 billion, further evidence of the carry trade. The BOJ is on hold at 0.3%, so the trade remains profitable, but positioning is at a level where a small catalyst could spark a massive unwind, reminiscent of past yen spikes in 2022 and 2007.

The yen continues to slide against the dollar, with bearish bets hitting a 52-week extreme as investors borrow cheaply in Japan to fund positions in higher-yielding US assets. This crowded trade is now a major vulnerability—any shock that causes the yen to strengthen could trigger a violent unwind, hurting risk assets globally.

Long-term takeaway · Reduce exposure to strategies dependent on a weak yen and consider taking profits on carry-trade beneficiaries; a reversal could erase months of gains quickly.

Taiwan · US

Taiwan capital flight raises tech concentration risks

iTaiwan's foreign flows over the past 5 days registered a net outflow of NT$286 billion, with a z-score of +0.65 (caution signal). The 20-day cumulative outflow is NT$207 billion. Yet the Taiwan dollar's z-score is 0.80 and improving (appreciating), meaning the selling is being absorbed without FX stress. Tech stocks in Taiwan are at historically high foreign ownership, and this rotation could be early-stage profit-taking. With the S&P 500 tech sector at the 98th percentile of 12-month returns, concentration risk is elevated.

Foreign investors have pulled over NT$200 billion from Taiwan's stock market in the past month, with a record single-day outflow flagged today. Despite this, the Taiwan dollar has remained stable, suggesting the selling is not panicked but rather a deliberate rotation out of tech-heavy positions. This could signal peak optimism in the global semiconductor cycle.

Long-term takeaway · Trim overweights in Asian technology and semiconductor stocks, and diversify into less crowded sectors like healthcare or industrials.

Historical Context

The current setup most closely echoes late 2007, when the yield curve flattened, volatility was low, and credit spreads were tight—signs of market complacency just before the financial crisis. Back then, the Fed was on hold, and risk assets had rallied for months, much like today. The key similarity is the extreme calm in equities alongside a crowded yen carry trade, both vulnerable to a sudden shock. The critical difference is that today's curve is positively sloped and the economy is still accelerating, not about to tip into recession. If this episode follows the 2007 pattern, we might see a liquidity-driven sell-off that begins in carry trades and tech before stabilizing—but strong growth could contain the damage.
